### Farmer Interview: Maize

We are able to sell our produce, but the amount buyers offer is often too low compared with the money and effort we invest in production. For example, if one acre produces about five bags of maize and the expected value is around GHS 1,000, after all the work involved, we may end up selling the produce for only about GHS 600. The final price depends largely on the buyer.

Sometimes, after harvesting, the market price is too low, so we decide to store the maize and wait until the price increases. However, keeping the produce for too long can cause it to spoil, resulting in losses. If we can find buyers immediately after harvesting, we may be able to make a better profit, but the prices offered at that time are often too low.

We would be very happy to have a platform that connects us directly with buyers and helps us sell our produce at profitable prices.

### Farmer Interview: Tomatoes

We put a lot of effort into producing tomatoes, but finding reliable buyers is a major challenge. Even when we take our tomatoes to the market, some buyers ask to take the produce and pay later. When we follow up for payment, they sometimes delay or refuse to pay on time, which creates financial difficulties for us.

We also spend a lot of money on agricultural chemicals, including chemicals imported from Spain and other countries. These high input costs contribute to our losses. We need support to connect with reliable buyers, reduce the cost of agricultural chemicals, and improve access to water for irrigation.

Because we do not have reliable buyers, we often have to transport our produce to the market ourselves. Once there, we sell to anyone willing to buy, sometimes at a very low price. We commonly sell at Donasi Market and Kasoa.

When buyers, especially market women, purchase our tomatoes and ask to pay later, they often tell us to wait until they have sold the tomatoes before they can pay us. This creates cash-flow problems for farmers.

If we cannot find buyers quickly, the tomatoes begin to spoil. Sometimes we give the produce away to people rather than allowing it to go completely to waste. When the tomatoes are badly spoiled, we have no choice but to throw them away.

We have about 22 acres of farmland where we grow different crops, including tomatoes, okra, maize, cassava, pepper, and others. Our major challenges include access to money, water, labour, and reliable farm helpers. When additional workers are needed, we sometimes have to call people from our hometown to come and help.

### Farmer Interview: Potatoes

Potato production is highly dependent on rainfall. However, excessive rainfall can also be a problem, as too much water can prevent the potatoes from growing properly.

### Market Seller 1: Janet

**Market:** Ashanti Market (Domenasi)

I usually buy my goods from Todi and Agogroshie markets. I do not know many farmers in the surrounding areas who can supply me directly.

Even when farmers from nearby areas come to sell to us, their prices are often higher than the prices of goods coming from Accra, despite the fact that those products have already required significant effort and transportation from the farm.

Because of this, it is sometimes better for me to travel to Accra to buy the goods.

If there were a platform that connected us with farmers and allowed us to purchase goods at affordable prices, we would be happy to use it. We already have customers who buy from us and pay later, provided they are trustworthy. However, some local suppliers put pressure on us to pay for the goods immediately.

### Market Seller 2: Filomena Mensah

**Market:** Ashanti Market (Domenasi)

We buy local tomatoes from Twelagua, Ada, and surrounding areas.

In the past, the surrounding areas produced tomatoes in much larger quantities. For example, in August, there used to be so many tomatoes that we sometimes did not know what to do with the excess. However, many farmlands have since been sold, and tomato production in the surrounding areas has decreased.

Farmers also come to the market to check prices before returning to arrange their produce for sale. For example, a female farmer may come to the market and ask about the price of a bucket of tomatoes. If the price is GHS 150, she may return home, rearrange the tomatoes, and later offer them to us for GHS 120.

Tomatoes spoil very easily. Some of the tomatoes we bought yesterday may already be spoiled, and we have to remove them. We are in business to make a profit, not a loss. If buying tomatoes from the surrounding areas continually results in losses, I would rather travel to Ada, where I might buy them for GHS 800, pay approximately GHS 100 for transportation, and sell them for a profit.

The price of tomatoes from the surrounding areas is sometimes higher than the price in Ada.

Tomatoes can begin to spoil within a week if they are not sold quickly. Farmers sometimes do not take this into consideration. As market sellers, we do not have refrigerators or proper cold storage facilities. If we had a storage facility with refrigeration, we could preserve the tomatoes for longer.

We also arrange the tomatoes carefully to make them attractive to customers. However, when the tomatoes are exposed to the sun, they spoil more quickly. Tomatoes from Ada are also generally firmer than the locally produced varieties.

### Key Challenges Identified

Across the interviews, several recurring challenges were identified:

- Low and unstable farm-gate prices.
- Limited access to reliable and direct buyers.
- Delayed payments from buyers and market sellers.
- High transportation costs.
- High costs of agricultural inputs and chemicals.
- Limited access to irrigation water.
- Labour shortages and high labour requirements.
- Poor storage and lack of cold-chain facilities.
- Rapid spoilage of tomatoes and other perishable produce.
- Limited access to timely and reliable market-price information.
- Reduced local production due to the loss of farmland.
- Weak connections between farmers and market sellers.

These challenges contribute to post-harvest losses, reduced farmer incomes, and inefficiencies throughout the agricultural supply chain. A digital platform that connects farmers directly with buyers, provides market information, coordinates transportation, and supports better planning could help address several of these problems.
